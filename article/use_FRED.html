Data-driven Market Insights using Federal Reserve Databases

Markets | November 14, 2025

For the astute investor, navigating the complexities of the stock and bond markets requires more than just intuition. It demands data-driven insights, and a treasure trove of such information lies within the Federal Reserve system. While the St. Louis Federal Reserve's FRED (Federal Reserve Economic Data) database is a well-known resource, a deeper dive reveals a wealth of equally valuable economic and financial indices developed and published by other Federal Reserve Banks. Understanding and utilizing these resources can provide a significant edge for those looking to optimize their portfolio strategies.

The FRED (https://fred.stlouisfed.org) database is one of the most powerful public‐data platforms for investors. It offers free access to hundreds of thousands of economic time-series (currently more than 841,000 series from over 118 sources). (FRED) Variables range from interest rates, yields, inflation, monetary aggregates, to regional employment, housing starts and beyond. Because it is comprehensive, timely and comes with graphing and download features, FRED gives the individual investor a “big data” platform previously available only to institutional economists.

For stock and bond investors, that means several opportunities:
Macro timing & risk signals. For example, the weekly St. Louis Fed Financial Stress Index (STLFSI) published by the St. Louis Fed is available via FRED and captures stress in interest-rates, credit spreads, volatilities and bank‐funding conditions. (Wikipedia -https://en.wikipedia.org) A rise in this index often foreshadows tighter credit conditions, slower growth, and risk‐off moves in equities or widening spreads in bonds.



Investor Takeaway:

  • Investors can monitor the STLFSI series on FRED to adjust portfolio risk—reducing equity exposure or lengthening bond duration when stress spikes, or cautiously rotating back when it subsides.


Analogous to STLFSI developed by Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis, other Federal Reserve Banks develop indices to create a single, clear signal from a vast number of complex, noisy economic data points. They are generally constructed as weighted averages of underlying component series, where the weights are statistically determined to represent the common movement or underlying factor driving all the components.

A notable one is the Chicago Fed National Financial Conditions Index (NFCI). The NFCI is a comprehensive, weekly measure of financial stress in the U.S. financial system.It combines 105 measures of financial activity, spanning money markets (e.g., TED spread {The TED spread is the difference between the interest rate on short-term U.S. Treasury bills and the interest rate on interbank loans}, commercial paper rates), debt and equity markets (e.g., corporate bond spreads, stock market volatility), and the traditional and "shadow" banking systems (e.g., commercial lending standards, leverage). The index is standardized to have an average value of zero and a standard deviation of one since 1971.
Positive values mean financial conditions are tighter-than-average (more stressed). Negative values mean financial conditions are looser-than-average (less stressed, often supportive of risk assets).



Investor Takeaway:

  • A sharp, sustained NFCI move above zero is a crucial signal for risk management, suggesting credit is tightening and markets are under duress.




Other than these indices, classic variables like the Industrial Production Index, inflation rate, unemployment rate or the 10-year Treasury yield are all easily accessible on FRED. Also available are: money velocity, commodity prices [e.g oil, gold], GDP, exchange rates and many other economic, financial, and monetary data series.





Investor Takeaway:

  • Set up a small dashboard in FRED or via its API (FRED provides an API for bulk download) to monitor a number of core series such as STLFSI, NFCI, 10-year yield, industrial production growth, corporate-bond spreads. You can define thresholds that trigger portfolio actions: e.g., if STLFSI > 0.7 standard deviations above historic mean and diffusion indices are below zero → reduce equity weight, switch to high‐quality bonds or shorten duration. Use regional Fed survey indices as early warning signs. These often move ahead of national data releases, giving a few days or weeks of lead time.


In summary, the FRED database and the suite of regional Fed indices provide investors with a rich toolkit. Rather than relying solely on earnings reports or market chatter, investors can monitor objective, high-frequency economic signals that feed into valuations, risk perceptions and the credit backdrop. The edge isn’t in predicting every turn—but in listening early and positioning accordingly. In volatile markets, those who ignore the data risk being late to the turn.
