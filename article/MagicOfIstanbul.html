A City of two continents - Istanbul connects Europe and Asia across the Bosphorus Strait



Magic of Istanbul, Türkiye

Travel | Apr 16, 2026

Istanbul arrived as a continuation of our Egyptian journey—another city shaped by time, layered civilizations, and geography that defines its soul. Flying in over the shimmering Bosphorus Strait, where Europe and Asia meet, it was immediately clear why this city has captivated human kind for centuries.
We settled into Cihangir, a hillside neighborhood in the Beyoğlu district known for its bohemian energy, narrow streets, and sweeping water views. Once home to diplomats and writers during the late Ottoman period, Cihangir today blends historic apartment houses with an artistic, almost village-like atmosphere. Cats lounge on sunlit stairways, cafés spill onto sidewalks, and small antique shops and bookstores invite slow wandering. Its elevated position offers some of the finest views of the Bosphorus, especially at sunset, when the water reflects shifting tones of gold and blue. Morning walks here quickly became ritual—strong Turkish coffee, fresh simit bread, and the quiet hum of a neighborhood waking up. From Cihangir, Istanbul feels personal, lived-in, and deeply human.



Cafes of Cihangir



Terrace View from Cihangir



From this vantage point, the city unfolds as a living map of history. Divided by the Bosphorus and the Golden Horn, Istanbul reveals distinct architectural identities shaped by successive empires.





Our first explorations took us to Sultanahmet, the historic peninsula where the skyline is defined by domes and minarets. At its heart stands the magnificent Hagia Sophia, completed in 537 AD under Emperor Justinian. For nearly a millennium, it was the largest cathedral in the world. Its vast central dome — seemingly suspended in air — remains one of the great engineering achievements of antiquity. Directly across the square, the Blue Mosque rises in graceful symmetry, its cascading domes and six minarets defining classical Ottoman architecture. Inside, over 20,000 Iznik tiles create a luminous blue interior that feels both grand and intimate.
Walking through Sultanahmet Square, once the site of the Byzantine Hippodrome, we were literally tracing the footsteps of Roman charioteers and Ottoman sultans.

No visit to Istanbul is complete without experiencing its markets, and the Grand Bazaar delivers both scale and atmosphere. Dating back to the 15th century, this labyrinth of over 4,000 shops reflects Istanbul’s long role as a trading hub between East and West. Nearby, the Spice Bazaar in Eminönü overwhelms the senses with aromas of saffron, cinnamon, and dried fruits—a reminder of the city’s place along ancient trade routes.



Spice Market -a historic marketplace that has thrived for centuries -



Crossing the Galata Bridge offered one of the most iconic perspectives of the city. Fishermen lined the rails while ferries moved steadily below, connecting continents in a daily rhythm unchanged for generations. On the northern side, the medieval Galata Tower—built by Genoese traders in 1348—stands as a reminder of Istanbul’s cosmopolitan past.

Galata Tower



Another day took us along the Bosphorus shoreline, where the architecture softens into elegance. The opulent Dolmabahçe Palace, built in the 19th century, reflects the Ottoman Empire’s shift toward European styles. Crystal chandeliers, grand staircases, and gilded halls signal a moment of modernization and ambition. Walking north through Beşiktaş toward Yıldız Park, we passed waterfront mansions—yalı—once home to Ottoman elites, their wooden facades facing the water in quiet luxury.



Dolmabahce Palace Gate



We also sought out Istanbul’s quieter cultural treasures, places that reward patience and curiosity. The Kariye Mosque, tucked away in the Edirnekapı neighborhood, offers a profoundly different experience from the grand imperial mosques. Originally built as a Byzantine church and later converted into a mosque, it is renowned for its extraordinary 14th-century mosaics and frescoes. These works depict scenes from the lives of Christ and the Virgin Mary with remarkable emotional depth and narrative detail. Gold ceramic pieces shimmer softly in dim light, while figures seem to move with surprising realism for their time. Unlike the monumental scale of Hagia Sophia, Kariye invites close looking—each panel a story, each wall a meditation on faith and artistry. Its relative quiet allows for a contemplative visit, often free from the crowds that fill Istanbul’s more famous landmarks.


Frescoes at Kariye Mosque



Further along the Bosphorus in the leafy district of Emirgan, the Sakıp Sabancı Museum presents another layer of cultural richness. Housed in the elegant Atlı Köşk (Horse Mansion), the museum combines art, history, and setting in a uniquely satisfying way. Its permanent collection focuses on Ottoman calligraphy—an art form that transforms language into visual expression. Delicate ink strokes, gilded manuscripts, and imperial signatures (tuğra) reveal a refined aesthetic tradition deeply tied to Islamic culture. The museum also hosts rotating international exhibitions, creating a dialogue between East and West that mirrors Istanbul itself. Outside, manicured gardens open to panoramic views of the Bosphorus, making it as much a place to linger as to learn.s.



Sabanci Museum at Emirgan



Equally compelling is the Istanbul Archaeological Museum, located near Topkapı Palace. This vast complex is often overlooked, yet it holds one of the most important collections of antiquities in the region. Walking through its halls is like tracing the deep roots of civilization—from Mesopotamia and Anatolia to the Hellenistic and Roman worlds. Among its most celebrated pieces is the Sarcophagus of the Mourning Women, a masterpiece of ancient funerary art discovered in Sidon. Equally striking is the Alexander Sarcophagus, intricately carved with scenes of battle and conquest, widely believed to depict Alexander the Great. The museum’s breadth underscores Istanbul’s historical position not just as a capital, but as a crossroads of cultures stretching across continents and millennia.



'Sarcophagus of Women" at Istanbul Archaeological Museum



One particularly meaningful evening was spent reconnecting with old friends from my high school days in Robert Academy ,Istanbul — a reminder that travel is not only about places, but about time, memory, and human connection.
Toward the end of our stay, an unexpected injury slowed our pace. Yet even in stillness, Istanbul reveals itself. Quiet mornings in neighborhood cafés, the distant call to prayer echoing across hills, and the steady movement of ferries on the Bosphorus offered a more intimate understanding of the city.



Istanbul is not just a destination—it is a convergence. Empires, religions, and continents meet here, layered one upon another yet still vibrantly alive. From Egypt’s ancient monuments to Istanbul’s living history, this journey traced the arc of civilization itself—an enduring story written in stone, water, and human ambition.

Traveler tips: The Istanbulkart is an essential, rechargeable smart card for public transport in Istanbul, offering access to metros, trams, buses, ferries, and funiculars. It costs roughly 165–200 TL for the card alone, with rides costing at least 30 TL per tap as of early 2026. Cards are available at airport kiosks, metro stations, and transport hubs.
